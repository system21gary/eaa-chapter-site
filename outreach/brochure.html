Where this came from

We talked about it this evening.
Here it is.

After we finished up I sat down to see how far I could get. Further than I expected — what follows is the whole site, working, not mockups. I'd rather show you something you can click than describe something I might build.

Before you turn the page, here's what's genuine and what's standing in, so you're reading it accurately.

Real

  • Every page works. Logins, permissions, the borrowing workflow — all actually built.
  • May's pancake breakfast, pulled from our AOPA listing, cartoon and all.
  • The field's details, our programs, the 501(c)(3) registration.
  • The structure — how events, builds, tools and members relate to each other.

Standing in until you tell me otherwise

  • The members are invented. Marta, Dave, Ken and their projects are all made up.
  • The artwork is machine-generated, holding a space for our photographs.
  • Officers, meeting nights and dues aren't in there — that's yours to give me.
  • I masked the personal mobile and email off the public event listing rather than republish them.

Nothing is committed. No domain registered, nothing published, nobody else has seen it. If the chapter would rather stay put, that's a fine answer and I'll not be offended — I enjoyed building it either way. But if it's useful, it's a running start rather than a blank page.

The other thing worth saying up front: this did not take long. That's not modesty — it's the actual point, and I'll come back to it near the end. The reason I could do this in an evening instead of over a winter is that the tooling has changed underneath us.

— Gary

eaa1699.org/members/locker/requests

The owner's inbox. Approve, decline, or mark returned, with a note each way. The tool's availability follows along on its own, so the locker never says "available" about something sitting in somebody else's hangar.

One detail worth pointing out. Neither member sees the other's email address until the owner says yes — the site sends the notification itself. That's the difference between a tool library and a member list that quietly becomes a mailing list, and it's the sort of thing that has to be decided while you're building rather than patched on afterwards.

The house rules sit on the same page — return it clean and on time, if you break it say so, don't lend a borrowed tool on to a third person, say thank you. Worth the board agreeing the wording; it's a two-minute change.

Who gets in
Joining

People ask. An officer approves. That's the whole job.

eaa1699.org/join

What they see. A short form — who they are, what they fly or are building, and a few sentences about themselves.

eaa1699.org/members/admin/applications

What an officer sees. The queue, with a badge in the bar showing how many are waiting. Approve — picking whether they're a plain member or an editor — or decline, with a note either way.

Nothing exists until somebody approves it

An unreviewed request isn't a half-made account cluttering the member list. It can't sign in, can't appear in the directory, can't own anything. The account is created the moment it's approved, and the person then sets their own password from a one-time link.

Nobody ever handles a password

Not at signup, not ever. The site keeps only a one-way scramble of a password, so no officer — and nobody who steals the database — can read one. Forgotten passwords get reset, never looked up.

Three levels, so no one person is the bottleneck. Members use the locker, keep build logs, read and comment on the blog. Editors also write posts and run the events calendar — that's the job for whoever already does the newsletter. Officers manage people. The last remaining officer account can't be removed, so we can't lock ourselves out of our own site.

An evening's work, and most of it was deciding things

I described what the chapter needed in plain English, the way I'd describe it to you. An agentic coding tool — I used CoderFlow, there are others — did the rest, and not the way autocomplete does it. It ran a loop:

It went and found things out on its own Our AOPA event page is one of those sites where the page source is empty and the content loads separately. It worked that out, found the underlying data, and pulled the real breakfast details and the cartoon. I didn't tell it how.
It worked out the structure, then built it Members, tools, loans, events, posts, build logs, applications — the data design, the screens and the permissions together, rather than as separate stages.
It tested itself and found real faults Wrote 79 checks, drove the site in a real browser, and caught six genuine defects — including an error page that came out blank, and a form that would have let anyone flood an officer's inbox.
It asked when it needed to "Should members request accounts, or should officers invite them?" I answered in six words and the whole flow changed to match. That's the part that felt least like using a tool.

What actually took the time was the decisions — should builds be public, should tool locations be visible to non-members, who approves new people. Those are chapter questions, not technical ones, and they're the ones I'd want your and the board's answers on. The building around them is close to free now.

Looking after members' details
The unglamorous part

Quick to build shouldn't mean careless

We'd be holding members' phone numbers, home airports and photographs of their hangars. In plain English, here's how that's handled.

Passwords can't be read by anyone

Only a one-way scramble is stored. No screen, no query and no officer can see a member's password. Forgotten ones are reset from a single-use link that expires within the hour.

Photos are stripped of location

Phones tag pictures with GPS coordinates. Every upload is decoded and rebuilt from scratch, which throws that away — so a photo of somebody's project doesn't publish their home address.

Members' details stay members-only

The directory and the Tool Locker are enforced in the database, not just hidden on the page. Suspend somebody and they're signed out immediately, not at the end of the week.

The known attacks are covered

Injection, cross-site scripting, forged form submissions, brute-forced logins, malicious file uploads. Ordinary hostile-internet hygiene, applied from the first line rather than after something happens.

Public forms don't leak

The "request an account" form answers the same whether or not an address is already a member — so it can't be used to work out who belongs to the chapter, or to flood an officer's inbox.

There's a record

Sign-ins, password resets, permission changes and content edits are logged. Visitors' IP addresses are kept only as a scrambled fingerprint — enough to spot an attack, not a pile of personal data.

Running it costs almost nothing

The whole site is one small program and one file on disk. No database server to look after, no monthly platform fee, no plugin subscriptions to lapse and break the site eighteen months from now when whoever set it up has moved away. It'll run on the cheapest hosting going — and because everything lives in that one file, backing up the chapter means copying it somewhere safe.
